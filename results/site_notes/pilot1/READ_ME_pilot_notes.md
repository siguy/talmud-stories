## Ketubot 10a (`ketubot-10a-11-11`)  — approved in October

**Why:** The Gemara has been discussing how far a husband is believed when he claims his bride was not a virgin, with earlier cases brought to Rabban Gamliel. This case shows a judge testing the claim physically rather than accepting it; the Gemara then asks if such tests work in Babylonia.

**Note:** A groom tells Rabban Gamliel that he found no blood on the wedding night, which suggested his bride had not been a virgin, while the bride insists she was a virgin. Rabban Gamliel asks for the cloth, soaks it in water and launders it, and finds several drops of blood. He tells the groom to go take possession of his acquisition, since she was a virgin and there is no cause for concern.

## Ketubot 17a (`ketubot-17a-4-4`)  — approved in October

**Why:** The Gemara is collecting examples of how Sages sang and danced to honor brides and scholars. This story shows a Sage's undignified wedding dancing being vindicated, and Rabbi Zeira's comment right after reads the pillar of fire as his reward for it.

**Note:** On the duty to bring joy to a bride and groom, Rabbi Yehuda bar Elai danced before the bride with a myrtle branch, calling her fair and attractive. Rav Shmuel bar Rav Yitzhak juggled three myrtle branches while dancing, and Rabbi Zeira complained that this demeaned Torah scholars. The passage ends by saying that when Rav Shmuel died a pillar of fire separated him from everyone else, a sign given to only one or two people in a generation.

## Ketubot 20a (`ketubot-20a-2-3`)

**Why:** The Gemara is debating whether witnesses can be contradicted when they are not present. Rav Nahman answers that two witnesses against two cancel out and the money stays with its owner, and the bar Shatya case is cited as the precedent for that rule.

**Note:** To settle a legal problem about witnesses who contradict each other, Rav Nahman points to an earlier case. A man called bar Shatya, who suffered bouts of insanity, sold his property. One pair of witnesses testified that he sold it while insane, and another pair that he sold it while sane. Rav Ashi ruled that the two testimonies cancel out and the property stays with bar Shatya, but only if he had inherited it from his fathers; if he had bought it himself, the sale stands.

## Ketubot 62b (`ketubot-62b-5-5`)  — approved in October

**Why:** The Gemara is discussing how often scholars must be home with their wives, and has said Friday night is their proper time. Like the story of Rav Rehumi just before it, this case warns of the danger of a scholar neglecting his wife for study.

**Note:** Yehuda, son of Rabbi Hiyya and son-in-law of Rabbi Yannai, usually came home every Shabbat eve, preceded by a pillar of fire that Rabbi Yannai saw. One day he stayed behind absorbed in study, and without the sign Rabbi Yannai told the family to turn his bed over as in mourning, assuming he had died. The text says that his words came true like a ruler's mistake, and Yehuda died.

## Ketubot 82b (`ketubot-82b-9-12`)

**Why:** The Gemara has been discussing the mishna's rule about the husband's property and the marriage contract, and here gives the history of why all of a husband's property is pledged to pay it.

**Note:** Rav Yehuda, and an older teaching that agrees with him, tell how the marriage contract came to work as it does. At first a bride was promised 200 dinars, or 100 for a widow, but men grew old without marrying. The Sages had the money kept in the bride's father's house, but an angry husband could too easily say: go to your marriage contract. Then it was kept in the husband's house, where rich women made it into silver and gold baskets and poor women into a chamber pot, and husbands still said: take it and leave. Finally Shimon ben Shatah ruled that a husband writes that all his property guarantees the payment.

## Ketubot 84b (`ketubot-84b-10-10`)

**Why:** The Gemara is collecting cases of creditors seizing property from orphans to pay their dead father's debts, and whether Rabbi Tarfon's view allowing this is followed. This case adds a reminder that Reish Lakish had overturned such a ruling.

**Note:** Members of the household of the prince, the head of the Jewish community in the Land of Israel, seized a maidservant belonging to orphans in an alley, as payment for a debt the orphans' father owed them. Rabbi Abbahu, sitting as a judge with two colleagues, told them the seizure was fine. Rabbi Abba, sitting with the judges, objected: were they flattering the prince's household? He reminded them that judges had once ruled this way, following Rabbi Tarfon, and Reish Lakish had reversed their ruling. The text does not say how the case ended.

## Berakhot 6b (`berakhot-6b-19-19`)

**Why:** Rav Huna has just said that one who prays behind the synagogue is called wicked, and Abaye limited this to someone who does not face the synagogue. The story shows how serious that offense is.

**Note:** A man prayed behind the synagogue without turning to face it. Elijah the Prophet passed by and saw him, appearing to him as an Arab. He said to him: is this how you stand before your Master? Then he drew a sword and killed him.

## Berakhot 7b (`berakhot-7b-26-27`)

**Why:** The Gemara has been quoting teachings of Rabbi Yohanan in the name of Rabbi Shimon ben Yohai; this exchange introduces another one, on the special power of communal prayer, which the discussion then supports with further verses.

**Note:** Rabbi Yitzhak asks Rav Nahman why he has not been coming to the synagogue to pray. Rav Nahman says he is too weak; when Rabbi Yitzhak suggests gathering a prayer group at home, Rav Nahman says it would be too much of a burden on the community. Rabbi Yitzhak then proposes that a messenger tell him when the congregation is praying so he can pray at the same moment. Asked why he is making such a fuss, Rabbi Yitzhak explains with a teaching from Rabbi Yohanan: the 'time of favor' in a verse from Psalms is the time when the congregation prays.

## Berakhot 16b (`berakhot-16b-10-10`)

**Why:** The mishna says Rabban Gamliel accepted condolences when his servant Tavi died, and the Gemara has just quoted a teaching that the mourning rites are not observed for slaves; this incident is the source of that teaching.

**Note:** When Rabbi Eliezer's maidservant dies, his students come to comfort him as they would a mourner. He tries to avoid them, going upstairs, then into the gatehouse, then into the banquet hall, and they follow him each time. Finally he scolds them for missing the hint and reminds them of his own teaching: the formal mourning rites are not held for slaves. Instead one says to the owner what one says when an ox or donkey dies, 'May the Omnipresent replenish your loss.'

## Berakhot 27b (`berakhot-27b-14-21`)

**Why:** The mishna says the evening prayer 'is not fixed', and the Gemara has just shown that the Sages disagreed whether it is optional or required; this is the famous dispute between Rabban Gamliel and Rabbi Yehoshua on that very question.

**Note:** A student asks Rabbi Yehoshua whether the evening prayer is optional or required and is told 'optional'; Rabban Gamliel, head of the Sages, tells him 'required'. Rabban Gamliel raises the question in the study hall, forces Rabbi Yehoshua to admit his view, and leaves him standing while he lectures. The angry assembly removes Rabban Gamliel and appoints the young Rabbi Elazar ben Azarya, whose hair miraculously turns white. The study hall is opened to all students, and Rabban Gamliel keeps attending. Later he visits Rabbi Yehoshua, is shocked to learn he earns his living as a blacksmith, and begs forgiveness. Rabbi Yehoshua forgives him and goes with Rabbi Akiva to help restore him; the story's final arrangement is not given here.

## Berakhot 30b (`berakhot-30b-24-24`)

**Why:** The discussion is about restraining excessive joy, with Sages rebuking each other for it; this is one of several incidents in which a host deliberately dampens the celebration at a wedding.

**Note:** Mar, son of Ravina, holds a wedding feast for his son and sees that the Sages there are getting carried away with merriment. To sober them, he brings out a valuable cup worth four hundred zuz and breaks it in front of them, and they become sad.

## Berakhot 42a (`berakhot-42a-4-4`)

**Why:** The Gemara is discussing which blessings apply to food eaten at the end of a meal; this incident is the first of several about when a meal counts as over, so that eating more needs a new blessing.

**Note:** Rav Pappa visits the home of Rav Huna, son of Rav Natan. After the meal is over, more food is brought out and Rav Pappa eats it without saying a new blessing. His hosts ask whether he does not hold that once a meal is finished one may not eat again without a blessing. He answers that the rule as correctly taught speaks of the table being removed, not of finishing the meal.

## Berakhot 49a (`berakhot-49a-21-21`)

**Why:** The Gemara has just reported Rav's ruling that one who forgot the Shabbat passage in Grace after Meals adds a short substitute blessing; this incident gives a conflicting report of Rav, which the discussion then reconciles.

**Note:** Giddel bar Manyumi is standing near Rav Nahman when Rav Nahman forgets to add the special Shabbat passage to Grace after Meals. Rav Nahman goes back and says the whole Grace again from the beginning. Asked why, he cites Rav's teaching, as reported by Rabbi Sheila: one who made this mistake goes back to the beginning.

## Berakhot 60a (`berakhot-60a-17-17`)

**Why:** The mishna calls a frightened prayer about a scream in the city a vain prayer, and the Gemara is praising trust over fear; this incident, like the one before it, teaches that one should fear only for one's Torah learning.

**Note:** Yehuda bar Natan is walking behind Rav Hamnuna and lets out a sigh. Rav Hamnuna asks whether he wants to bring suffering on himself, quoting Job: 'that which I did fear is come upon me.' Yehuda replies with the verse 'Happy is the man who fears always,' and Rav Hamnuna answers that that verse is about fear of forgetting Torah.

## Bava Metzia 23b (`bava_metzia-23b-8-8`)

**Why:** The Gemara has just asked whether the place an item is found can serve as an identifying sign that lets the owner reclaim it. Rav's ruling looks like evidence that it cannot, until Rabbi Abba gives a different reason.

**Note:** A man found some pitch lying near a winepress and asked Rav whether he could keep it. Rav told him to take it. When Rav saw the man still hesitating, he told him to share it with Rav's own son Ḥiyya, to show he was certain it was not stolen property. The Gemara first suggests Rav ruled this way because the spot where an item is found does not count as an identifying sign, but Rabbi Abba explains otherwise: grass had grown through the pitch, so it had clearly lain there long enough that its owner must have given up on it.

## Bava Metzia 24b (`bava_metzia-24b-10-10`)

**Why:** The Gemara is collecting rulings in which Sages let a finder keep lost meat and goods found in public places, following Rabbi Shimon ben Elazar's view; these two cases continue that series right after the similar case of Rabbi Ḥanina's slaughtered goat on the same road.

**Note:** Two short cases of found property. Rabbi Ami found slaughtered young birds on the road between Tiberias and Tzippori and asked what to do; the reports differ on whether he asked Rabbi Asi, Rabbi Yoḥanan or the study hall, but the answer was: take them for yourself. Rabbi Yitzḥak Nappaḥa found a skein of thread used for weaving nets, asked Rabbi Yoḥanan or the study hall, and got the same answer, because he found it in a place where crowds pass.

## Bava Metzia 44b (`bava_metzia-44b-4-4`)

**Why:** The Gemara is debating whether gold coins count as currency or as a commodity in relation to silver. This incident is brought to show that Rabbi Ḥiyya held gold is currency, though the discussion then rejects the proof on other grounds.

**Note:** Rav borrowed gold coins from the daughter of Rabbi Ḥiyya. By the time he came to repay, gold coins had risen in value, and Rav worried that returning the same number of coins, now worth more, would count as paying forbidden interest. He asked Rabbi Ḥiyya, who told him to repay her the same number of sound, full-weight coins. The Gemara reasons that this ruling works only if gold coins count as money, not as a commodity whose price rises and falls.

## Bava Metzia 69b (`bava_metzia-69b-5-5`)

**Why:** Rava has just ruled that a person may accept a fee for asking someone else to make a loan, since it is payment for talking, not interest. This incident is an example of that ruling in practice.

**Note:** Abba Mar, son of Rav Pappa, used to take pans of wax from wax makers and then ask his father to lend them money. The Sages complained to Rav Pappa that his son was taking interest. Rav Pappa replied that this kind of interest is allowed: the Torah forbids only payment that goes from the borrower to the lender, and his son was being paid for putting in a word for them.

## Bava Metzia 84a (`bava_metzia-84a-8-9`)

**Why:** The Gemara has been describing the bodies and beauty of the Sages, especially the beauty of Rabbi Yoḥanan. This story continues that theme, and the discussion goes on to give another source for why Joseph's descendants are safe from the evil eye.

**Note:** Rabbi Yoḥanan, famous for his beauty, used to sit at the entrance of the ritual bath. He explained that Jewish women coming out after their monthly immersion should see him first, so that they would have children as beautiful and as learned in Torah as he was. The Rabbis asked whether he was not afraid of the evil eye. He answered that he was descended from Joseph, whom the evil eye cannot harm, citing a verse about Joseph as Rabbi Abbahu read it.

## Bava Metzia 85b (`bava_metzia-85b-4-4`)

**Why:** The Gemara has just told of a Divine Voice saying Reish Lakish analyzed Torah like Rabbi Ḥiyya but did not spread it as he did. This exchange contrasts brilliance in argument with the work of keeping Torah alive, and Rabbi Ḥiyya's account of his teaching follows.

**Note:** Rabbi Ḥanina and Rabbi Ḥiyya used to argue over Torah, and each would tell the other he was no match for him. Rabbi Ḥanina boasted that if the Torah were ever forgotten by the Jewish people, he could rebuild it through his own sharp reasoning. Rabbi Ḥiyya answered that he was the one making sure the Torah would never be forgotten in the first place. The Gemara goes on to let him explain how he did it.

*Drafter flag: The story ends on Rabbi Ḥiyya's claim; his explanation of what he actually does (sowing flax, writing scrolls, teaching children), which completes the exchange, is outside the story span.*
